## Title

**"The 60/40 Test: Is Your Retirement Money Managed — or Just Riding the Market?"**

Reasoning: YouTube titles get found by search intent, not browsed like a mailer gets
opened — there's no DRA list or Meta targeting doing audience-selection here, so the
title has to do that job itself. "60/40," "retirement money," and "managed" are terms
someone already worried about their own allocation actually types into a search bar.
The question format previews the video's actual mechanism (a test, not a lecture) rather
than promising generic "retirement tips." Deliberately not reusing the card's headline
("Three ways retirement money can be positioned...") — that's a cold-mailer teaser built
to survive a three-second glance at a mailbox; a YouTube title is competing in search
results and suggested feeds against other specific, answerable questions, and needs to
read as one itself.

## Thumbnail concept

Whiteboard photo, not stock imagery or a polished graphic — matches the "live build"
authenticity the format is named for. Three simple hand-drawn containers, left to right.
Middle one has a large "?" with a red circle around "2008" and a down arrow next to it.
Justin mid-gesture, marker in hand, caught mid-explanation rather than posed. Text
overlay, minimal: **"MANAGED?"** No shock-face, no red arrows screaming "crash," no dollar
signs — the thumbnail's job is to signal "this is a real test, not a sales video,"
matching the tone of everything downstream of it.

## Target length: 9–11 minutes

This is one mechanism — one disqualifier, one realization — not a full financial-house
build. Padding it toward a longer "authoritative" runtime works against both halves of
the voice this brand runs on: Godin's generosity means giving the whole argument cleanly
and stopping, not manufacturing watch-time; Miller's clarity means one idea, delivered
so a half-listening viewer gets it, not several idea threads competing for attention.
Retention on educational finance content drops hard past the point where the promised
question gets answered — this video's promise (is your 60/40 managed or not) is fully
answered by minute 8–9; anything past that should be the close and CTA, not new
material. If the beats below run long in the edit, cut from the build's explanatory
padding (Beat 3), not from the pause (Beat 4) or the naming moment (Beat 8) — those are
the two beats doing the actual work.

## The script

### COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:15

**WHITEBOARD:** Three empty containers already drawn, left to right, evenly spaced, no
labels, no color yet. Justin is standing at the board when the video starts — no logo
sting, no "hey everyone, welcome back," no channel intro.

**JUSTIN** (to camera, gesturing at the three containers, already mid-thought):

> "There are three places retirement money can sit. Only one of them actually has a
> plan for what happens when the market drops — not just where the money's invested,
> what happens if it falls. I'm going to draw you all three. And if you think you
> already know which one your money is in — don't take my word for it by the end of
> this. Check it yourself. That's the whole video."

*Why this opening:* no claim about the viewer, no promise about outcomes, no "most
people" anywhere. It states what the video does (shows three containers, lets the
viewer check their own answer) and nothing it can't back up. Fifteen seconds is a hard
ceiling here on purpose — first spoken sentence has to survive someone skimming with the
volume half-up.

---

### BEAT 1 — The two easy boxes — 0:15–1:00

**WHITEBOARD:** Justin labels the first container **"CAN'T LOSE VALUE"** and the third
**"FULLY EXPOSED."** The middle stays blank — no label, no question mark yet, just
empty.

**JUSTIN:**

> "This one" *(taps first box)* "can't lose value. It's not going anywhere, up or down,
> no matter what the market does. This one" *(taps third box)* "is fully exposed —
> whatever the market does, this does. Up, down, all of it. Most retirement money I see
> is in one of these two, or split between them."

**Note for shoot:** "Most retirement money I see" is Justin describing his own direct
observation from his own practice, not a claim about the general population — same
posture as the class-content.md client anecdote. Keep it there, don't let it drift into
"most people have" phrasing, which is the disallowed construction. If Justin's own
delivery on camera starts to sound like a population claim rather than "here's what I
personally see," that's a take to reshoot, not a line to cut — the fact underneath it is
fine, the phrasing is what has to stay disciplined.

---

### BEAT 2 — Staging the middle box — 1:00–2:30

**WHITEBOARD:** Justin writes **"60/40"** inside the still-unlabeled middle container —
60% stocks, 40% bonds, written out.

**JUSTIN:**

> "Here's the box everybody thinks they're standing in. If I asked you, 'what's your
> plan for when the market drops,' and your answer is 'I'm 60/40 — some stocks, some
> bonds' — that's this box. That's what people mean when they say 'managed.' A mix.
> Some growth, some safety, blended together.
>
> So let's test it. Not with an opinion. With a real fund — the Vanguard Balanced Index
> Fund. It's about as straightforward a 60/40 as exists. You can look it up yourself
> when this is over. Let's see what it actually did."

*Why the fund gets named here:* a checkable, named fund is a feature, not a risk — it's
the specificity that makes the test land as evidence instead of a rhetorical number.
"You can look it up yourself" is doing real work in the StoryBrand sense: it hands the
viewer the means to verify the guide's claim rather than asking them to trust it.

*Why this stays a "test," not an assertion yet:* this is the direct build-out of
Justin's classroom move — he shows a result and asks, he doesn't tell them their mix
isn't a plan. Nothing here states a conclusion about 60/40; it names the candidate and
promises evidence.

---

### BEAT 3 — Running the test — 2:40–3:30

**WHITEBOARD:** Justin writes **"2008"** next to the "Vanguard Balanced Index Fund /
60/40" label in the middle box.

**JUSTIN:**

> "2008. The Vanguard Balanced Index Fund — 60% stocks, 40% bonds. Before I show you
> the number, I want you to actually answer this, not just keep watching:
>
> Is that money being managed? Or is it riding the markets?"

Do not write a number on the board yet. This is the question, asked and held — the
reveal is Beat 5.

---

### BEAT 4 — The engineered pause — 3:30–3:50 (target: ~15–20 seconds on screen)

This is the hardest craft problem in the piece, per the brief, so here's the actual
solve, in three layers — don't cut any of the three, they're each doing different work:

**Layer 1 — Justin names the device out loud, so silence reads as intentional, not
technical.** Immediately after asking the question:

> "I'm going to stop talking for a second. In the actual class, this is where the room
> goes quiet — everybody's doing the math on their own account. Take that same second."

This matters because it's true (it's a direct description of what happens in his own
class, not a claim about the viewer) and because it gives the viewer explicit permission
to use the silence instead of experiencing it as dead air or a stream glitch.

**Layer 2 — Hard cut to a clean shot of the board.** Camera cuts from Justin's face to a
static, well-lit shot of the whiteboard: the three containers, the middle one still
unlabeled, "60/40" and "2008" sitting there with nothing answered yet. No Justin in
frame. Light ambient room tone only — no music sting, nothing that reads as "moving on."

**Layer 3 — On-screen text, timed, not spoken.** A single line fades in under the board
shot and holds:

> **Managed? Or riding the markets?**

Hold on this shot for 4–5 seconds after the text finishes fading in (full beat is
15–20 seconds including Justin's spoken lead-in). Then hard cut back to Justin at the
board.

*Why this works on camera where raw silence wouldn't:* a room's silence is legible
because everyone can see everyone else thinking. A camera has no equivalent — silence on
a single locked face reads as buffering. Naming the device, cutting to a static image
with the question restated in text, and holding a specific timed duration replaces "a
room full of people visibly thinking" with the one thing video actually has: a clean,
unhurried shot the viewer's eye can sit on. Recommend shooting two hold lengths (12
seconds and 20 seconds) in the edit bay and picking based on how it actually plays —
this is a genuine unknown until it's cut, not something to lock from the script alone.

---

### BEAT 5 — The reveal — 3:50–4:35

**WHITEBOARD:** Justin returns, writes the actual figure under "2008."

**JUSTIN:**

> "Here's what it actually did." *(writes "**−22.12%**")*
>
> "That's not a small dip. That's a real, sharp drop — in the box people call 'managed.'"

Short beat, no restated question yet — the second data point in Beat 6 is what earns
the full "one more time" restatement.

---

### BEAT 6 — The second year — 4:35–5:20

**WHITEBOARD:** Justin writes **"2022"** next to a second line under the same fund.

**JUSTIN:**

> "One more year, because I don't want this to look like a fluke. 2022. Same fund.
> This is the year stocks *and* bonds fell together — the exact year a 60/40 mix isn't
> supposed to be able to have, if the bonds are actually protecting anything."
>
> *(writes "**−16.90%**")*
>
> "Down again. Two different years, two different kinds of bad market, same mix, same
> result.
>
> So — one more time. Managed? Or riding the markets?"

Second, shorter pause here — 2–3 seconds, no cutaway needed this time, since Beat 4
already trained the viewer on what a pause from Justin means. Just a hold on his face,
no cut.

**Why two years and not more:** 2022 is the year that makes the argument directly
rather than by implication — bonds falling alongside stocks is the exact failure mode
"I have bonds, that's my downside plan" doesn't expect. Two years is enough to show
this isn't a one-time fluke without stacking enough data points that it starts reading
as a forecast rather than a description of what already happened — three or more
consecutive down years risks exactly that, which is why 2000 and 2020 are both left out
here rather than added for volume.

---

### BEAT 7 — Landing the realization — 5:20–6:30

**JUSTIN**, still at the board, gesturing between the middle box and the number:

> "So look at that middle box again. You wrote '60/40' in it because that's what
> managed is supposed to look like. But a mix that drops that hard in a bad year — is
> that a plan for the downside? Or is that just... riding the market, with some bonds
> along for the ride?
>
> If your honest answer is 'that's not really being managed, that's just riding it' —
> then that box" *(taps the still-unlabeled middle container)* "isn't actually where
> your money is. Even though you've been calling it that."

*Compliance note baked into the delivery:* this stays a question the viewer answers for
themselves, never a statement Justin makes about their specific account — matches the
Socratic structure that's non-negotiable per class-content.md and keeps this off the
individualized-advice line. If a take on set drifts toward Justin telling the viewer
what they have rather than asking, that take doesn't get used.

---

### BEAT 8 — Naming the third box — 6:30–7:30

**WHITEBOARD:** Only now does Justin write the actual label into the middle container:
**"MANAGED WITH A PLAN FOR THE DOWNSIDE."**

**JUSTIN:**

> "This is the box that's actually built to do something specific when the market
> drops — not just sit there and absorb less of the hit because some of it's in bonds.
> An actual plan for what happens on the way down, decided ahead of time, not
> discovered the hard way in a year like 2008.
>
> In the class I teach, I call this the **Blue bucket**. Most of the retirement money
> I actually see — in my own practice, in the accounts people bring me — is in the
> first box, the third box, or a mix that looks like this one and isn't."

*Why "Blue" gets named here and not held back:* per brand-voice.md's 2026-09-16 rule,
YouTube live-builds are explicitly a warm-audience surface — the name gets earned by the
six minutes of setup the viewer just sat through, the same way it's earned by 40 minutes
in the room. This is the single moment in the whole script where the word appears; it's
withheld everywhere before this beat on purpose, so it lands as a reveal, not a label
dropped in the cold open.

---

### BEAT 9 — The full picture — 7:30–8:15

**WHITEBOARD:** Full board now shows all three labeled: **CAN'T LOSE VALUE / MANAGED
WITH A PLAN FOR THE DOWNSIDE / FULLY EXPOSED.**

**JUSTIN**, stepping back so the full board is in frame:

> "Three boxes. Can't lose value. Managed with an actual plan for the downside. Fully
> exposed. That's it. That's the whole test. Go look at your own accounts and ask the
> same question I just asked about 2008 and 2022: is this being managed, or is it
> riding the markets? You don't need me in the room to answer that one honestly."

---

### CLOSE & CALL TO ACTION — 8:15–9:45

**Argued CTA, not the default one:** No Calendly exists yet and Meta is parked, so
there's no funnel step downstream of this video to send anyone to — and I'd argue that's
fine, not a gap to paper over. Godin's whole premise is that a YouTube CTA can be softer
than a paid ad's, because the viewer already spent eight minutes of unpaid attention;
asking for a phone call at the end of that is a bigger ask than the trust built so far
has earned, especially as video one of a series. **Primary spoken CTA: subscribe, framed
as "there's a next question," not "there's a next sales step."** Secondary CTA:
description-only, low-key, for the smaller number of people who are ready to talk now.
**Deliberately not sending anyone to the in-person class here** — the entire strategic
reason this video exists is to push the Blue realization up the funnel past the point
where a physical room is required; routing the CTA back to the class would quietly
undo the one thing this piece is for. That's a real tradeoff (see "where this is weak,"
below), but I think it's the right one for video one specifically.

**JUSTIN**, to camera, board still visible behind him:

> "If that test just changed how you'd answer 'is my money actually managed' — that's
> the whole point of this channel. I'm going to keep building this out, one piece at a
> time, the same way I build it in the class. Subscribe if you want the next one.
>
> And if you want to talk about your own accounts before then — my number's in the
> description. No pitch, just a conversation."

**ON SCREEN, end card (last 5 seconds):** Subscribe button graphic + "Next: What
actually goes in the middle box." No countdown timer, no "limited," no urgency device of
any kind — consistent with the 2026-09-12 hard wall on urgency framing.

version by default a second time.

**Video 3 — "What actually goes in the middle box."** The natural next question a
viewer of Video 1 is left with: he named the box, described it in one line ("built to
do something specific when the market drops, decided ahead of time"), but never said
what that actually looks like. This is where the series can go further than the class
does in forty minutes — not by naming a product or making a recommendation (that line
doesn't move), but by walking through, conceptually, what "a plan decided ahead of
time" means as a category of approach versus a mix that just happens to contain bonds.
This is the StoryBrand "here's the plan" beat for the series — Video 1 is the problem
and the guide's credibility, Video 3 is where the plan itself gets explained.

## Where this is weak

**The question you asked directly: is a cold YouTube viewer close enough to the class
audience for this to work, or is this built for a different person?** Honest answer:
closer than a cold mailer or a cold Facebook impression, but not the same person, and
the gap is real enough to name rather than assume away.

The class audience is filtered twice before Justin ever speaks: once by an institution
(a college, credit union, or employer lending its own credibility to "come learn
about retirement"), and once by the DRA mail list's demographics. A cold YouTube viewer
who finds this through search has filtered *themselves* — they typed "60/40 portfolio
retirement" or something close to it, which is arguably a sharper self-selection than
responding to a mailer, since it's driven by their own live question rather than an
invitation showing up in their mailbox. That's the case for this working at all: search
intent is doing real targeting work that Meta's Special Ad Category currently can't do
for the FB test, and that the video doesn't need to pay for.

But that's only true of *search* traffic. YouTube's suggested/browse feed will also
surface this to people with no specific retirement question at all — anyone half-watching
general personal-finance content, any age, any account size — and there's no DRA list,
no Meta targeting, and no institutional gate filtering that traffic the way it filters
the class or the mailer. The title and thumbnail are the only targeting mechanism this
piece has, and I built them to lean hard into retirement-specific language for exactly
that reason — but a title can't do what an audience list does. Practical consequence:
expect view counts that include a real share of people who were never going to become
clients, and don't read raw views as a lead-quality signal — subscriber growth on
retirement-adjacent language and comment content ("I'm 60/40 too, didn't think about
it that way") are better signals than view count, closer to the "meetings booked per
piece" discipline this workforce already applies to the mirror card, not "views per
piece."

The other real gap: the class has forty minutes to work a specific viewer's actual
objection live — "but what about my bond ladder," "my advisor actually does hedge some
of it" — the same ceiling I flagged on the printed card in three-buckets-theme-v1.md
Section 6. A nine-minute video has even less room for that than a card does; it has to
pick one false positive (60/40) and let it stand alone, same as the card, with the same
tradeoff. I think that's fine for what Video 1 is actually for — produce the same doubt
the class produces, not resolve every objection — but it means this video's job is
identical to the card's job, not a superset of it. The thing the video adds isn't more
persuasive completeness; it's reach without a geographic fence and without a marketing
dollar spent per view, which is the actual strategic point Justin raised this week. I'd
be more worried about this gap if the video were trying to replace the class outright —
it isn't, it's trying to extend the same realization past the driving-range limit the
class and the card both have.

**One more thing worth saying plainly, since it's a real tradeoff and not just a risk:**
routing the CTA away from the class (see the close, above) is the right call for what
this video is strategically for, but it means this piece does nothing to fill the
existing class's declining attendance — if Justin wants this video to also feed the room,
that's a different, second CTA decision, and I'd argue against making it the primary one
here. Worth being explicit that I made that call rather than defaulting to it.

**Production risk, not a strategy risk:** the engineered pause in Beat 4 is a real
solve on paper, but it's untested — I don't have a prior video in this channel to check
it against, and "does a 15–20 second held shot with on-screen text actually hold
attention or invite a skip" is an editing-bay question, not a scriptwriting one. Shoot
it as written, but don't be surprised if the cut needs a shorter hold than the range I
gave once it's actually in the timeline.
