## 0. Where this lives, and where I chose not to put it

Isla's brief floated three candidate moments: the review meeting, right after the plan
is delivered, or the correction email. I picked one primary anchor and one recurring
echo, on purpose — a ritual scattered across three places isn't a ritual, it's three
different asks that happen to share a topic.

**Primary anchor: the end of the plan-delivery meeting** (the same meeting as the
Financial House Analysis walkthrough). This is the single highest-trust moment in the
relationship — the client has just seen their own three-bucket picture, understood
something about their own money they didn't before, and is feeling relief or clarity,
not being sold to. That's the moment worth asking in, once.

**Recurring echo: the annual review meeting**, at the close, in a lighter form. Not a
new ask each time — a restatement of the standing offer, so the ritual repeats across
the life of the relationship instead of firing once and never again.

**Deliberately not the correction email.** I thought about this one and I'm pushing
back on it rather than drafting it in. During a correction, the reader is anxious —
that's the entire premise of why the email works as a market-moment touch. Asking
someone for a referral in the same message that's meeting them at a scared moment
reads as opportunistic even if the copy is careful, and it cuts against the "no urgency
framing" instinct Ed has already enforced on that piece twice. The correction email's
job is to be useful to the person who got it, full stop. If a client forwards it to a
worried friend on their own initiative, that's already covered as a normal, low-risk
act in `.claude/memory/growth-channels.md` — a client sharing something useful is
different in kind from Justin building a referral ask into the trigger. Leave it out.

## 1. Why this shape, not the "give me some names" version

The version most advisors run — "if you know anyone who could use my help, send them my
way" — asks the client to do the actual work of identifying and pitching a stranger.
Almost nobody follows through on an open-ended ask like that, not because they don't
want to help, but because it takes effort with no clear next step. Miller's rule
applies here too: the client is the hero of their own referral, not the unpaid
salesperson for Justin's business. The ritual below is built to minimize what the
client has to do:

- **Ask for one name, not a list.** One name is answerable on the spot; "anyone you
  know" is a homework assignment.
- **The client doesn't have to say anything promotional.** They give a name and
  permission; Justin does the actual outreach. This also keeps the client's role as a
  warm introduction, not an endorsement — see the compliance note in section 3.
- **No script the client is expected to recite.** An optional forwardable line is
  offered below, but it's optional and factual, not a testimonial-shaped pitch they're
  handed to repeat. This is the same lesson from the "Do you have Blue?" kill in
  `.claude/memory/growth-channels.md` (2026-09-12) — a canned line handed systematically
  to referrers is exactly the pattern that got killed there, both because it doesn't
  work outside the classroom context and because it edges toward an undisclosed
  referral arrangement. I kept this ritual narrow on purpose to avoid repeating that.

## 2. The ritual, in Justin's own words

### Moment 1 — End of the plan-delivery meeting, spoken

Timing: after the three-bucket walkthrough is done, the client has reacted, and the
conversation is naturally wrapping up — not stapled onto the start or middle of the
meeting.

> "Before you head out — one thing that would genuinely help me, if you're open to it.
> Is there one person you know — maybe three to five years out from retirement — who's
> still piecing this together on their own, the way you were before today?
>
> I'm not asking for a list. Just one name, if one comes to mind. If you're comfortable,
> just let them know I do this same free plan for anyone — no cost, no obligation — and
> that you're the one who mentioned my name. I'll take it from there.
>
> And if no one comes to mind right now, that's completely fine — this isn't something
> I need an answer to today."

**What makes it easy to act on:**
- One name, not a list — a specific, answerable ask instead of an open-ended one.
- The client doesn't have to compose anything or make a pitch — just permission to be
  mentioned.
- Justin does the actual outreach once introduced, and only with the client's stated
  permission — the client isn't asked to set up a meeting or explain the plan
  themselves.
- An explicit, genuine out ("that's completely fine") — this has to actually be low-
  pressure, not low-pressure-sounding.

### Optional — a line the client can send themselves, only if they'd rather reach out directly

Offered, never required. Kept strictly factual/descriptive — no evaluative claim about
Justin or the outcome he produced for them (see compliance note, section 3):

> "Hey — thought of you. My advisor does a free plan review, no cost, no pressure. Want
> his number?"

### Moment 2 — Annual review meeting, spoken, lighter restatement

Timing: at the close of each subsequent annual review, after the substantive part of
the meeting is done.

> "Same standing offer as always — if anyone in your world comes to mind who's still
> trying to figure this out on their own, I'm glad to do the same free plan for them
> that I did for you. No need to think of someone right now — just keeping the door
> open."

This is intentionally the same offer restated, not a new ask each year. The point is
that it's a known, predictable moment in the relationship — the client isn't caught off
guard by it, and it doesn't accumulate pressure the way an escalating ask would.

## 3. Compliance constraints applied (from Ed's precedent) and what still needs his eyes

Checked against `.claude/memory/compliance-precedents.md` (2026-08-25 gift/event entry
and its 2026-08-25 refinement) before drafting a word:

1. **Nothing of value changes hands for an introduction.** No gift, no fee, no
   discount, no service credit — confirmed nowhere in this draft. If Justin ever wants
   to thank a referring client afterward, that's a separate decision with its own
   compliance path (anti-rebating check if the client has insurance/annuity business
   with Trulip) — not something to fold into this ritual by default.
2. **No client is positioned as a promoter.** The client gives a name and permission;
   Justin does the outreach. The optional forwardable line is factual/descriptive
   ("does a free plan review, no cost, no pressure"), not an evaluative endorsement
   ("he's great," "he saved us money") — flagging this distinction explicitly for Ed
   to confirm the line stays on the right side of it. Per the 2026-08-25 refinement to
   the precedent: the promoter question specifically requires a client introducing a
   prospect to something of value the advisor is funding — there's no funded event or
   thing of value here, which is the cleanest version of this ritual could be, but the
   refinement also flags that a *repeated, predictable pattern* of client-sourced
   introductions can itself start to look like an undisclosed referral arrangement
   even without a gift attached. This ritual is by definition repeated and
   predictable — that's the whole design. **This is exactly the edge case Ed's own
   precedent says needs a second look, not a clean pass by default; flagging for Ed
   explicitly rather than assuming the no-gift structure clears it on its own.**
3. **No testimonial-shaped copy.** Neither script above asks the client to describe
   their own results, say what Justin did for them, or make any claim about outcomes.
   Both scripts describe the service offered (a free plan, no cost), not its effect.
4. **Recordkeeping.** Every referral conversation — the ask, the name given, the
   outreach that follows — needs logging the same way any other client correspondence
   does (Rule 204-2). This is an operational habit for Justin's office, not something
   this draft builds.

**What's still open for Ed, stated plainly:** item 2 above is the one I can't resolve
by drafting carefully — whether a scripted, repeated referral ask (even gift-free, even
non-testimonial) constitutes a promoter arrangement is the same category of question
Ed flagged as needing outside counsel on the CPA-handoff and retirement-celebration
concepts. I'd rather send this to Ed with that flag attached than assume the absence of
a gift settles it.

## 3a. Ed's determination (2026-09-16)

**Copy: clean.** Both scripts hold the line Sloan built them to hold — one name, not a
list; permission to be mentioned, not a pitch to deliver; the optional forwardable line
is factual ("does a free plan review, no cost, no pressure"), not evaluative, and makes
no claim about outcome or performance. No testimonial-shaped language anywhere. No gift,
fee, discount, or service credit changes hands in either direction, in either script or
in the surrounding text. Nothing here needs a copy fix.

**Recordkeeping: confirmed as Sloan wrote it, with one addition.** Every referral
conversation (the ask, the name given, permission granted, the outreach that follows)
gets logged under Rule 204-2, same as any other client correspondence — that's right as
drafted. Adding the one piece not yet in the draft: if Justin's outreach to the referred
name happens by text, that text has to run through an archiving-capable business
platform, not a personal phone — same standing rule already applied to the mirror's
texting touches. The name-and-permission conversation itself (spoken, in the meeting)
gets logged as a dated note the same way any other meeting content would be.

**The promoter question — near the line, not over it, not clear of it. Here's exactly
why, and exactly what to ask counsel.**

The 2026-08-25 precedent's two-element test (a client introducing prospects, to (b) an
event/thing of value the advisor is funding) is genuinely not met here — there's no
funded event and no compensation of any kind, to the referring client or the referred
prospect. On that test alone, this reads clean, and Sloan's read to that point is
correct.

But that two-element test was built for gift/event fact patterns, and this isn't one —
it's a scripted, repeated, permanent verbal solicitation built into every plan-delivery
and annual-review meeting, forever, for every client. Two features push this past a
spontaneous, uncompensated referral and into genuinely gray territory:
1. **It's systematic by design**, not incidental — the same 08-25 refinement says a
   repeated, predictable pattern of client-sourced introductions can start to look like
   an undisclosed referral arrangement even without a gift attached. This ritual's whole
   design goal is to be that predictable.
2. **Justin authors the words the client is invited to use** (the optional forwardable
   line) — that's the adviser generating content for a client to relay to a prospect,
   which sits closer to the adviser soliciting/directing testimonials than pure
   spontaneous word of mouth, even though the line itself is factual, not evaluative,
   and even though using it is optional.

The Marketing Rule's promoter/testimonial provisions are triggered most clearly by cash
or non-cash compensation, which is absent here — that's the strongest thing in this
ritual's favor. But the rule's definition of "testimonial" also reaches an uncompensated
client referral of a prospective client, and a scripted, adviser-authored, systematized
version of that referral is a materially different fact pattern from an occasional,
spontaneous one — the same distinction this precedent file already drew for the
CPA/attorney systematic-handoff question ("unmodified card, no compensation, no active
vouching = distribution, lower risk; any compensation or vouching = promoter"). This
ritual has no compensation, but it does invite a mild form of vouching (an optional
line describing the service), delivered systematically. That combination is why I'm
calling it **near the line, not over it, and not clear of it** — it doesn't have the
clean "no" that the pure two-element gift/event test would suggest on its own.

**Exact question for outside counsel:** *Does a firm-wide, systematized, scripted
client-referral request — built into a recurring meeting structure (plan-delivery and
every annual review), where no cash or non-cash compensation, discount, gift, or credit
is exchanged with either the referring client or the referred prospect, and where the
client's only actions are (a) naming one person and granting permission to be mentioned,
and (b) optionally relaying a short, factual, non-evaluative line drafted by the
adviser — constitute a "testimonial" or "endorsement" under Rule 206(4)-1 triggering the
disclosure requirements of subsection (b), and if so, what disclosure can practically
travel with an informal spoken referral or a client's private text to a friend?* This is
counsel's call, not mine — I'm not resolving it by assuming the absence of a gift
settles it, same as Sloan flagged.

## 4. My honest read

This is a good use of Justin's existing habits, not a new invention — he already gets a
few referrals a quarter without any ritual at all, which tells me the relationship
already produces goodwill; the problem is that goodwill currently has no reliable
moment to turn into an action. Anchoring it to the plan-delivery meeting is the right
call because that's the one moment in the relationship that's structurally identical to
what converts strangers in a classroom — a person just realized something true about
their own money. I'd resist any pressure to also wire this into the correction email or
into every single client touchpoint; a ritual that fires everywhere isn't a ritual,
it's a reflex, and reflexive asks are exactly what erodes the trust this whole practice
runs on. One clean anchor, one honest echo, repeatable for years — that's the whole
play. The one real unresolved question is the promoter one in section 3, and it's not
mine to resolve by writing better copy.
