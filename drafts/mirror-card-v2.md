   nothing in place to limit how far they fell?

6. **Ed's format recommendation (2026-09-12):** one 8.5x11 sheet, bi-fold to 8.5x5.5,
   four panels — (1) cover/hook, (2) five questions, (3) scoring key + CTA + Justin's
   first-person close + signature, (4) disclosures only, nothing else on that panel.
   Reasoning: the disclosure block above runs roughly 300-350 words. At a size an
   audience that skews 55+ can actually read without strain — 8pt minimum, and larger
   is better, not smaller — that's easily 2.5-3.5 column-inches of dense text on its
   own. A 5x7 or 6x9 flat card has one back side to hold scoring key, CTA, close,
   signature, AND that block; something gives, and disclosure text is exactly what
   gets shrunk or crowded when things don't fit, which is the wrong thing to
   compromise on. A dedicated fourth panel means the disclosures never compete for
   room with the CTA or the close, and never get typeset smaller just to make them
   fit — readability at that font size is itself part of whether the disclosure
   counts as adequately given, not just a production preference. A tri-fold was
   considered and set aside: narrower panels (~3.5-3.75in) would cramp the five-
   question panel, which needs its own breathing room for five yes/no rows with
   checkboxes, and a tri-fold's natural third panel is usually a reply/address panel
   this piece doesn't need. An insert (disclosures on a separate slip in the mailer)
   was also considered and rejected: it's a second physical piece that can be
   discarded, separated, or never read by the recipient, which undercuts the point
   of disclosing at all, and it complicates the recordkeeping story — one artifact
   "as mailed" is simpler to log and defend later than two. One folded sheet keeps
   it a single artifact, keeps production simple (one sheet, one fold, standard
   equipment), and still reads as a "card" if printed on heavier stock rather than
   flimsy paper.
7. **"No pitch" as a recordkeeping matter (2026-09-12):** Justin's personal
   commitment is necessary but not sufficient on its own. The card makes a printed,
   factual claim about how the first meeting will run ("no pitch," "no obligation").
   Under Rule 204-2, an advertised claim needs to be substantiated by a record, not
   just true in practice — and if the claim is ever tested (an exam, a complaint,
   an internal question a year from now about what "no pitch" meant operationally),
   an unwritten intention doesn't answer that. Recommend two things, not one: (a) a
   short internal policy note in Trulip's actual compliance file / written
   supervisory procedures — not this repo's memory system, which is workforce
   institutional memory, not a substitute for the firm's own compliance
   recordkeeping — stating the rule plainly (first meeting following this card is
   no-pitch; product conversation happens only in a separate, later meeting), dated
   to when the card goes out; (b) a line in whatever script or agenda Justin or
   anyone else uses to run that first meeting, so the rule is actually operational
   for whoever's in the room, not just a promise on paper. When the card is finalized
   and logged (marketing-recordkeeping-logger), that log entry should reference
   where this internal policy note lives, so the substantiation trail is complete.
