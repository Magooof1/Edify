## The cover check, run first, per standing instruction

**Does the cover tell a stranger what they get?** The two prior rejected covers each
failed a different half of this — one proved credibility with no hook
(BrokerCheck-first), one posed a problem with no named payoff. Everything below is
built to pass both halves in the same glance: a hook that's actually about the
reader's own experience, and a subhead that says plainly what happens if they act on
it.

## Why this source, and where I'd push back on the brief that sent me here

I agree the story is the right source, for the reason given: it's the only material
in Part 1 that doesn't require the reader to already hold a tax person, an insurance
person, a 401(k), or any prior relationship with Justin. It requires nothing except
having wondered, at some point, whether the person handling your money is actually
any good — which is closer to universal than any framing this workforce has tried.

**Where I'd push back, and it's a real one, not a formality.** The story's emotional
engine is a third party's grief — a family friend, husband just died, asking for
help. That's true, it's why the course exists, and it's genuinely moving material.
But it is *her* loss, not the reader's, and Jayme's whole discipline says a cover has
to be instantly, unmistakably about the person holding it. A story about someone
else's tragedy risks answering "is this about me?" with "no — it's about somebody
else's terrible year." I've built the cover around the part of the story that *is*
universal (the reader's own mailbox, the reader's own unasked question) and pushed
the widow's specific circumstance — real, and doing real work — back to Panel 2,
where there's room to tell it with the care it needs rather than compress it into a
three-second glance. See "What I'm least sure about" below; I don't think this is
fully resolved.

## PANEL 1 — Cover

**Photo:** `justin-portrait-open-collar-hi-res.jpg`. Same reasoning as before — open
collar over tie is doing real work against the approachability barrier on anything
cold, and this piece is built entirely around that barrier, more than either prior
draft was.

**Headline:**

> **You've probably had a dinner seminar show up in your mailbox. This isn't that.**

Justin's own line, verbatim, from the actual opening of the class — the one place in
Part 1 that talks about the medium it's about to be printed in.

**Subhead, small, under the photo:**

> *Ask me anything about your retirement — the same way a friend once did. Free. No
> pitch, ever.*

**What this cover does and doesn't promise, on purpose.** It doesn't name "about an
hour" the way the prior cover did. That wasn't an oversight — it's downstream of the
open venue problem. The prior draft's "about an hour, free, nothing to sign" borrowed
language from the Step 2 Form's in-office visit, which is exactly the ask Justin's
travel rule says shouldn't be step one for a cold stranger. Until the mechanism is
decided, I'd rather promise less specifically (ask me / free / no pitch) than promise
a duration or setting that might not survive the offer decision. See Panel 3 below
for what I need from that decision.

**Argument for this cover, against two I considered and rejected:**

1. **Rejected: the widow's exact question as the cover headline.**
   `"Justin, I know you work in the industry. Is there anything you could help me
   with?"` This is genuinely the strongest single line in Part 1 — found language,
   completely unlike anything competing in the mailbox, and it hands the reader
   permission to ask a question a lot of people on this list are silently sitting
   on. I moved away from it as the *cover* for the reason above: it's someone else's
   crisis, not the reader's, and a stranger scanning it for three seconds has to do
   an extra step of work (whose voice is this, why does it matter to me) before the
   payoff lands. That's a cost a cover can't afford. It's too good to cut — it opens
   Panel 2 instead, where the reader has already been told "this is about your
   mailbox, and therefore about you" and has a beat longer to sit with someone
   else's story before it turns into an argument about theirs.

2. **Rejected: "only a handful, out of 2,000" on the cover.** This is the story's
   real credibility engine, and I considered leading with it. Compliance is why I
   didn't: Justin's own qualifier — *"it doesn't mean there was anything wrong with
   the other people... some focused on retirement planning, some did not"* — has to
   travel with that claim or it reads as disparagement of every advisor not in the
   handful. Fitting the claim and a qualifier that makes it non-disparaging into a
   cover panel that also has to get opened is a bad trade: either the qualifier gets
   compressed to nothing (unsafe) or it eats the cover's word budget (slow). Panel 2
   has room to do both properly. The cover doesn't need the number to work — it
   needs the reader to feel *addressed*, and "you've probably had a dinner seminar
   show up in your mailbox" does that without touching the compliance-sensitive
   claim at all.

3. **Rejected: the tornado as cover art.** A photograph of storm damage to a house is
   arresting in the abstract, but on its own it's oblique — it works live because
   Justin asks the room a question and waits for an answer; on paper, without that
   exchange, it risks becoming exactly the "diagram where an image belongs" failure
   mode this role exists to resist, just swapped for a photo instead of a line
   drawing. It's a strong device for Panel 2, where the copy can carry the question
   Justin actually asks. It's not, on its own, a hook.

**What passing the cover check actually rests on here.** The subhead is the whole
payoff: permission (ask me anything), price (free), and risk (no pitch). If those
three land, the cover clears Isla's rule. If a reader doesn't believe "no pitch," the
whole cover fails regardless of headline — which is why that promise needs the
operational backing flagged below, not just the words.

## PANEL 2 — The correction (the story, the qualifier, and the tornado)

**Headline:**

> **A friend of mine asked me exactly that.**

Picks up the cover's subhead directly — no re-explanation needed, the reader already
knows what "that" refers to.

**Body:**

> I spent about ten years traveling the country, working alongside financial
> advisors — Edward Jones, LPL, Raymond James, the banks. By the time I stopped
> counting, I'd worked with roughly 2,000 of them.
>
> Then a family friend came to me. Her husband had just died. She was retiring on
> her own for the first time, and she didn't know who else to ask. "Justin," she
> said, "I know you work in the industry. Is there anything you could help me
> with?"
>
> I told her I'd think it over.
>
> Out of those 2,000 advisors, there was only a handful I'd actually have sent her
> to. Not because the rest were bad at their jobs — some just didn't focus on
> retirement. Some managed money in ways I wouldn't want for somebody about to
> depend on it. That's the whole difference. Not good or bad. Right for this or not.
>
> That's the real question behind every seminar invitation that lands in your
> mailbox: is the person on the other end of it actually one of the ones worth
> trusting? Nobody answers that for you. I built a way to answer it for yourself.
>
> I start the same way in every class I teach. What's the first thing to fail on a
> house in a tornado? Not the foundation. The roof goes first. Then the walls.
> What's left standing is whatever was actually built to hold weight.
>
> That's where we start with your retirement, too — not income, not what you'll
> leave behind. The foundation. Get that part right, and the rest tends to hold.

Roughly 230 words. Tighter than it looks on the page — five short beats (career,
the ask, the number, the correction, the device) rather than one continuous
argument. Still worth naming plainly: this is already most of Justin's "30-60 words
per panel" real-estate-mailer target, spent on one panel. I'd rather flag that now
than have it discovered at layout.

**The disparagement qualifier, placed where the brief requires it.** *"Not because
the rest were bad at their jobs — some just didn't focus on retirement... That's the
whole difference. Not good or bad. Right for this or not."* This travels immediately
with "only a handful," in the same paragraph, not in a footnote or a later panel. Not
padding — it's the sentence that keeps the preceding one from being a disparaging
comparative claim.

**Small photo callout, bottom of panel:** `justin-whiteboard-three-buckets.jpg`
(already Ed-cleared for unlabeled use on the prior draft, 2026-09-23 — "unlabeled
circles with a caption about the conversation's length do not describe Green").
Caption: *"This is what building the foundation actually looks like."* Optional —
cuts if the panel's already full; the tornado paragraph does the work without it.

## PANEL 3 — The offer: deliberately left open

**Not drafted.** Per the brief's constraint and Justin's own travel rule (`"people
will come to NKU or Harrison within ~10 miles; they drive to my office only after a
first meeting has shown value"`), the mechanism this opening hands off to isn't
settled, and I'm not writing offer copy that guesses at it.

**What I need from the offer decision for this opening to pay off:**

1. **The first step can't be "come to Crestview Hills."** The cover's whole premise
   is low-stakes permission — "ask me anything." Asking a cold stranger to drive to
   an advisor's office as their very first move contradicts that in exactly the way
   the prior draft got flagged for.
2. **It can't be the 3.5-hour class either, presented as the only front door.** That
   reintroduces the size-of-ask problem `growth-channels.md` and Taleb's notes
   already have on record — the thing that's been filtering people out isn't
   information, it's the size of the yes being asked for. A three-and-a-half-hour
   Saturday is a bigger yes than "ask me anything."
3. **My best guess at what fits, offered as a guess, not a recommendation I can
   stand behind without Justin's read:** a phone call. The retired mirror card
   offered a twenty-minute call and `mailer-v2`'s own file flagged that this "may
   have been quietly solving a problem this piece reintroduced." A call keeps the
   promise the cover already made (ask me anything, no pitch, no cost) without
   committing anyone to travel before they've gotten anything out of it.
4. **Whatever it is, the CTA should stay in "ask" language**, not "schedule a
   consultation" or "book an appointment" — the cover trains the reader to expect an
   answer to a question, not a sales meeting.
5. **The no-pitch promise on the cover needs the operational backing that's already
   an open item elsewhere in this practice** (`drafts/no-pitch-policy-note.md` —
   needs Justin's signature). This piece is not the first to need it, but it is
   making the promise more explicitly and earlier — on the cover itself, not three
   panels in — so if that policy note stalls, this piece's central claim is
   unsupported before a reader gets past the fold.

## Compliance risks flagged for later — not reviewed, not handled

Justin held this back from Ed. Nothing below has been checked. Flagging so Isla and
Ed have a real starting list rather than a blank one when it does go through:

1. **The friend's story is real, identifiable-adjacent, third-party content — and
   she was never Justin's client.** This isn't a testimonial (she isn't praising his
   work), but it is someone's actual grief being used as marketing material. The
   2026-08-25 precedent on client-derived story content ("Not a testimonial... but
   it is client-derived material and would need Ed's eyes before it ever left the
   room") is the closest analog, and she isn't even a client, which may put this in
   a category the compliance log hasn't seen yet. **This is the item I'd most want
   Ed to look at first,** both as a consent question and as a taste question — see
   "least sure about," below.
2. **"Only a handful, out of 2,000" is a specific, precise personal-career claim.**
   Same category as the 2.22% 401(k)-fee figure already flagged elsewhere in this
   project — needs a substantiation-file basis (years traveled, rough count
   methodology) even though it's Justin's own first-person history, not a
   population statistic.
3. **Naming Edward Jones, LPL, Raymond James by name, in print, for the first
   time.** Spoken in a live class this passed Portfolio Medics review as part of the
   deck. A cold mailed piece is a different exposure — worth Ed confirming rather
   than assuming the class-deck clearance travels.
4. **"You've probably had a dinner seminar show up in your mailbox."** Almost
   certainly fine — it's addressed to the reader's own probable experience, not
   framed as a population statistic — but it's new to print, in this exact
   construction, on a piece already carrying two other flags. Worth a second set of
   eyes rather than assumed clean by analogy.
5. **"No pitch, ever" is a printed, mailed representation now sitting on the cover
   itself**, not three panels deep. Same standing rule as every prior "no pitch"
   claim in this project's precedent file: it needs a real, dated, enforced
   operating record behind it (Rule 204-2), not just the copy.
6. **The insurance geofence question is untriggered by this draft as written** —
   neither panel describes Green. Flagging the reasoning, not asserting it as
   settled: if the offer (Panel 3) or the landing page it points to ever describes a
   principal-protected feature, the funnel inherits the OH/KY fence the way the
   prior draft's mirror destination did, regardless of what this cover says.

None of the six above is resolved. All six are Ed's to work through once Justin

## What I'm least sure about, in order

1. **Whether pulling the widow's story back to Panel 2 solves the third-party-grief
   problem or just relocates it.** Panel 2 still tells a stranger's story about her
   husband's death, in a cold mailer, to sell a course. I've tried to handle it with
   respect — brief, no exploitation of detail, immediately in service of a
   non-disparaging point about vetting advisors — but I'm not confident that's
   enough. If Justin reads Panel 2 and feels the same way he felt about the
   BrokerCheck cover — *this isn't about them, it's about me* — that's a real signal
   worth taking seriously, not a taste call to override.
2. **Whether "only a handful, out of 2,000" reads as reassuring or alarming to a
   stranger who has no idea whether their own advisor made anyone's list.** The
   brief calls this "the setup for a disappointing finding," correctly. But a
   disappointing finding with no offer attached to it yet (Panel 3 is open) risks
   landing as pure anxiety with nowhere to go. I don't have a way to test that from
   here, and it's exactly the kind of thing the offer decision needs to resolve
   quickly once it's made.
3. **Whether Panel 2's ~230 words is still too many**, against Justin's stated
   objection to word count on the last draft. I've compressed hard, but this panel
   is doing five jobs (career context, the ask, the number, the qualifier, the
   device) that the rejected card spread across three. I'd want this read aloud
   against a stopwatch before I'd call it right.
4. **Whether the tornado bridge survives being cut down to two sentences.** The
   class-script note calls it "the most memorable thirty seconds" precisely because
   it's Socratic — Justin asks, the room answers, then he tells them. On paper I've
   turned it into a rhetorical question with no pause for an answer. It may already
   be the "diagram where an image belongs" failure in prose form rather than in
   pixels. I don't have a way to test that from here either.
5. **Whether deferring the number to Panel 2 costs more pull than I think it does.**
   It's possible "2,000 advisors, only a handful" is simply the more magnetic hook
   and belongs on the cover regardless of the qualifier problem — solvable with a
   smaller-type asterisk line rather than full prose. I talked myself out of that
   version for the reasons above, but it's a real candidate I didn't build, not one
   I tested and rejected on evidence.
